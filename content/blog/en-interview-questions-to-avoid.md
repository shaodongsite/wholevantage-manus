# Entering the U.S. Market: What Questions Should You Avoid Asking Candidates in Interviews?
Compliance boundaries and a guide to asking the right questions in cross-cultural interviews

In the previous article, we discussed how to choose recruiting channels based on the type of role when hiring the first local employees in the U.S., and how to establish an effective recruiting process.

Once those channels are in place and candidates begin reaching the interview stage, companies expanding into the U.S. face another critical step: the interview.

After entering the U.S. market, many Chinese companies discover that recruiting platforms can be learned and job descriptions can be improved, but interview communication is where cultural differences and compliance risks are most likely to become apparent.

In Chinese workplaces, some questions that seem like a natural way to “get to know a candidate” — such as questions about family circumstances, age, or personal plans — may be seen as part of building a relationship.

In the U.S. hiring environment, however, some of these questions may concern protected characteristics, so companies need to exercise great caution.

1. Three types of questions most likely to cause problems in U.S. interviews

One of the core principles of compliant hiring in the U.S. is: Hiring decisions must be based on job-related factors, rather than a candidate’s personal characteristics.

1) Questions about personal background
These include questions about age, marital status, family, children, and place of birth. Such questions may be relatively common in Chinese workplaces, but in U.S. hiring they can easily be viewed as unrelated to the job and may create discrimination risks.

2) Questions about identity and personal circumstances
A company may confirm a candidate’s work authorization, but it should avoid asking about personal background unrelated to the role, such as national origin or place of birth.

3) Questions about health and special needs
A company may discuss the role’s essential duties and work requirements, but it should not ask about a candidate’s health or disability in advance.

💡 An important principle: Do not ask, “Who is this person?” Instead, ask, “Does this person have the abilities needed to perform this role?”

2. For companies expanding into the U.S., the greatest risk often comes from interview habits

Many risks do not originate with U.S. hiring managers. They stem from the continuation of management habits at headquarters. For example:
- Treating an interview as an opportunity to learn extensively about a candidate’s personal circumstances
- Judging a candidate’s likely stability based on family, age, or background
- Allowing different interviewers to ask questions at will, without a common standard
- Having both Chinese headquarters and the U.S. team participate without defining interview boundaries

These practices may be taken for granted in China, but in a U.S. hiring context, they make it harder for a company to explain its hiring decisions.

For that reason, U.S. recruiting requires companies to establish in advance: Standardized interview questions; Clear criteria for evaluating candidates against the role; and Training for managers who participate in hiring.

3. What should U.S. interviews focus on?

A compliant interview does not mean “asking no questions.” It means focusing questions on job-related factors.

1. Past experience - What the candidate has done before and what responsibilities they held.
2. Abilities and results - How the candidate has solved problems and what value they have created.
3. Approach to work - How the candidate communicates, collaborates, and handles challenges.
4. Fit for the role - Whether the candidate’s experience, abilities, and career direction meet the company’s needs.

A truly effective interview is not about learning about a candidate’s private life. It is about determining: Is this person suited to the role, and can they help the company solve real business problems?

Conclusion: A compliant interview is a company’s first introduction to its overseas employer brand

For companies expanding into the U.S., a professional, compliant interview experience that respects candidates can reduce potential employment risks. It is also an important first step in building an employer brand in the U.S. talent market.

Hiring the first employees is not just about finding people to get the work done. It is also a way for a company to show the U.S. market what kind of company it is. No detail is too small when expanding overseas; compliance supports efficiency.
